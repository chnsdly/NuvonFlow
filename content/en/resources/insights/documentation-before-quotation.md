Technical documents are often discussed after product and price, but they can affect product choice, supplier scope, review time, and delivery expectations. A quotation that does not define documents may appear complete while leaving important project work unresolved.

## Documents affect the supply scope

Typical requirements may include:

- Product data sheets and approved technical schedules
- Dimensional, assembly, or interface drawings
- Installation, operation, and maintenance instructions
- Material, conformity, origin, or compliance declarations
- Inspection, test, calibration, or packing records
- Document numbering, language, format, and revision control

Not every project requires every item. The point is to identify the expected set before commercial comparison.

## Timing matters

Some documents are required with the quotation, some for technical approval, and others only with delivery. Mixing these stages can cause avoidable delays or make suppliers quote different scopes.

A controlled document list should identify the required item, submission stage, review status, language, file format, and whether revised or final versions are expected.

## Make commercial comparisons clearer

When document requirements are included in the RFQ, quotations can state what is available, what is project-specific, what requires confirmation, and what is excluded. Buyers can then compare more than product price and avoid discovering documentation gaps after order placement.
