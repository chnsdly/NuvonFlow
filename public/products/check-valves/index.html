Selection Focus

  • Protects flow direction

    Check valves reduce reverse flow risks in pump discharge, dosing, transfer, and utility lines.

  • Orientation matters

    Horizontal, vertical up-flow, low-pressure, and pulsating service can require different styles.

  • Velocity-sensitive

    Oversizing or low-flow operation can cause chatter, wear, pressure loss, or unreliable sealing.

Product Details

Where check valves fit in a system

Check valves are installed where reverse flow could damage equipment, contaminate a line, drain a system, reduce metering accuracy, or disrupt process stability. They are common on pump discharge lines, chemical dosing systems, transfer lines, water treatment piping, wastewater equipment, and utility service.

A check valve should be selected around the actual flow condition, not only the pipe size. Flow velocity, cracking pressure, pressure drop, pump behavior, line orientation, solids, pulsation, and chemical compatibility can all affect performance.

Typical service situations

Service area Why check valves are considered Review points
Pump discharge Reduce reverse flow after pump stop Flow rate, pump curve, closing behavior, pressure surge risk
Chemical dosing Protect metering pump and injection line stability Cracking pressure, pulsation, chemical compatibility, low-flow behavior
Water treatment lines Maintain flow direction in process or utility piping Orientation, pressure drop, valve style, maintenance access
Corrosive transfer Prevent backflow between tanks, skids, or process areas Wetted material, seal material, solids, cleaning fluid exposure

Selection notes

The correct style depends on how the line actually operates. Swing, ball, spring-loaded, wafer, and dosing-service check valves can behave very differently. Low-flow or intermittent service may need special attention because the valve may not open fully or may chatter.

Cracking pressure matters in dosing and low-pressure service. A valve that needs too much opening pressure can affect metering pump performance or stop low-flow operation. A valve with too little closing force may not prevent reverse flow reliably in all orientations.

For corrosive service, review every wetted part: body, seat, spring, ball, hinge, disc, seal, and any fasteners exposed to the fluid or environment. If solids are present, consider clogging, abrasion, and maintenance access.

Common documents to request

Depending on project scope, request:

  • Product datasheet
  • Dimensional drawing
  • Material and wetted-part information
  • Pressure-temperature information by product series
  • Cracking pressure or spring information when applicable
  • IOM or maintenance instructions
  • Inspection or test documentation if required

RFQ checklist

To prepare a more reviewable inquiry, include:

  • Pipe size and connection standard
  • Flow rate or pump capacity
  • Operating pressure and allowable pressure drop
  • Installation orientation and flow direction
  • Continuous, intermittent, or pulsating operation
  • Media, concentration, temperature, and solids content
  • Preferred material, if known
  • Quantity, destination, and required documents

Final style, cracking pressure, material suitability, and pressure drop should be confirmed against the selected product series and actual operating conditions.
